Protecting safety without abandoning compassion

How to Set Boundaries With Someone Who Has an Addiction

Loving someone with an addiction can make it difficult to know where compassion ends and self-protection begins. Healthy boundaries are not punishment. They are clear decisions about what you will do to protect your safety, stability, finances, home, children, and emotional well-being.

What Is a Boundary?

A boundary is a clear limit around what you will accept, participate in, or provide.

It focuses on your own choices and actions.

A boundary is not:

  • A threat.
  • A punishment.
  • An attempt to control another adult.
  • A guarantee that they will change.
  • A way to force someone into recovery.

You cannot control whether another person drinks, uses drugs, lies, or seeks help.

You can control what you allow in your home, how your money is used, when you end a conversation, and what steps you take when a situation becomes unsafe.

Demand: “You need to stop drinking.”

Boundary: “I will not stay in the house with you when you are drinking and becoming aggressive.”

The first statement tells someone else what they must do.

The second explains what you will do.

Why Boundaries Matter

Addiction affects more than the person using substances.

It can disrupt:

  • Family routines.
  • Finances.
  • Housing.
  • Employment.
  • Parenting.
  • Trust.
  • Physical safety.
  • Emotional stability.

Without boundaries, family members often begin organizing their entire lives around avoiding the next crisis.

They may lend money they cannot afford to lose, tolerate abusive behavior, cover up consequences, or repeatedly abandon their own needs.

Boundaries interrupt that pattern.

They create clarity where addiction has created confusion.

Boundaries Are Not the Same as Abandonment

Many people fear that setting limits means they no longer care.

But you can love someone deeply while refusing to participate in behavior that is harming both of you.

“I love you, and I am willing to help you find treatment.”

You can also say:

“I will not give you cash.”
“I want a relationship with you.”

You can also say:

“I will end the conversation when you threaten or insult me.”

The goal is not to withdraw love.

The goal is to separate love from unlimited access to your time, money, home, vehicle, or emotional energy.

Decide What Needs a Boundary First

You do not need to create rules for every frustrating behavior at once.

Start with the areas creating the greatest risk or instability.

These often include:

  • Driving while impaired.
  • Using substances around children.
  • Violence, threats, or intimidation.
  • Bringing drugs or unsafe people into the home.
  • Repeated demands for money.
  • Stealing or damaging property.
  • Verbal abuse.
  • Substance use inside your home.
  • Contact while heavily intoxicated.
  • Failure to follow agreed childcare arrangements.

Focus first on safety.

Smaller concerns can be addressed later.

Make the Boundary Specific

Vague boundaries create confusion.

Statements like:

“Things need to change.”

or:

“I cannot keep doing this.”

may be emotionally true, but they do not explain what will happen next.

A clear boundary usually answers three questions:

  1. What behavior is the concern?
  2. What will you do if it happens?
  3. Are you realistically prepared to follow through?

Clear boundary: “If you arrive at my home intoxicated, I will not let you inside.”

Unclear boundary: “You need to respect me more.”

Boundary Examples for Money

Money is one of the most common sources of conflict when addiction is active.

You may feel pressure to prevent eviction, buy food, cover a phone bill, pay legal fees, or fix another emergency.

Possible boundaries include:

“I will not give you cash.”
“I can buy groceries, but I will not transfer money.”
“I can pay the treatment provider directly.”
“I will not pay debts caused by drinking or drug use.”
“I will not allow you to use my credit cards or banking information.”

These boundaries allow you to respond to legitimate needs without providing unrestricted resources that may support substance use.

Boundary Examples for Your Home

Your home should remain emotionally and physically safe.

Possible boundaries include:

“Drugs and alcohol are not allowed in my home.”
“You cannot bring people into my home without asking me.”
“If you use substances in the house, you will need to leave.”
“You may stay here only while following the safety agreement we discussed.”
“I will not allow threatening behavior, property damage, or violence in my home.”

Housing boundaries should be realistic and clearly communicated.

Do not promise consequences you are unwilling or legally unable to carry out.

Boundary Examples for Communication

Addiction-related conversations can quickly become hostile, manipulative, or exhausting.

You are allowed to limit how and when those conversations happen.

“I will talk with you when we are both calm.”
“If you insult or threaten me, I will end the call.”
“I will not argue with you while you are intoxicated.”
“I will respond tomorrow when we can have a clearer conversation.”
“I will not answer repeated calls in the middle of the night unless there is a genuine emergency.”

Ending an abusive conversation is not cruel.

It is a reasonable response to harmful behavior.

Boundary Examples Involving Children

Children should not be placed in situations where substance use creates danger, instability, or fear.

“I will not leave the children in your care when you are intoxicated.”
“You may not drive the children after drinking or using drugs.”
“Substances cannot be used around the children.”
“If you arrive impaired for a visit, the visit will not happen.”
“I will contact emergency services if the children are in immediate danger.”

When children are involved, safety should take priority over avoiding conflict.

Boundary Examples for Driving and Vehicles

Impaired driving creates an immediate risk to the person, passengers, and everyone else on the road.

“You may not use my car after drinking or using drugs.”
“I will not ride with you when I believe you are impaired.”
“I will arrange another ride, but I will not give you the keys.”
“If you take my vehicle while impaired, I will contact the police.”

Do not physically place yourself in danger trying to take keys from someone who is violent or unpredictable.

Seek outside help when necessary.

Communicate Boundaries During a Calm Moment

Whenever possible, do not introduce a complicated boundary during intoxication, withdrawal, or a screaming argument.

Choose a quieter moment when both of you can understand what is being said.

Keep the message direct.

“I care about you, and this situation is affecting both of us. I need to be clear about what I will do going forward.”

Then state the boundary without a long speech.

You do not need to list every past mistake to justify it.

Use Calm, Direct Language

A boundary does not need to sound aggressive to be firm.

Try to avoid:

  • Insults.
  • Labels.
  • Threats you do not mean.
  • Long arguments about the past.
  • Attempts to diagnose the person.
  • Statements designed to humiliate them.

Instead, use clear language:

“I am not willing to do that.”
“This is what I can offer.”
“If that happens, this is what I will do.”
“My decision is not changing.”

Calm does not mean uncertain.

Expect Resistance

When a family pattern changes, the other person may react strongly.

They may say:

  • “You do not love me.”
  • “You are abandoning me.”
  • “You are making everything worse.”
  • “You are the only person who can help.”
  • “This is your fault.”
  • “I promise this will be the last time.”

Their distress may be real.

But their reaction does not automatically mean the boundary is wrong.

“I understand that you are upset. I care about you, and my decision is still the same.”

Do Not Debate the Boundary Endlessly

After a boundary is stated, the conversation may become an attempt to wear you down.

You may feel pressured to explain it again and again.

A brief response is often enough:

“I have explained what I am willing to do.”
“I know you disagree.”
“The boundary is still the same.”

Repeatedly defending your decision can turn a clear limit into a negotiation.

Follow Through Consistently

A boundary that is never enforced becomes a request.

If you say you will end abusive calls but remain on the phone for another hour, the pattern does not change.

If you say you will not provide cash but repeatedly make exceptions, the boundary becomes unclear.

Consistency does not require perfection.

You may struggle or make exceptions during a crisis.

If that happens, reassess and begin again.

Ask yourself:

  • Was the boundary realistic?
  • Did I have the support needed to maintain it?
  • Was I afraid of the other person’s reaction?
  • Do I need a safer or more practical plan?

Avoid Consequences You Cannot Carry Out

It may feel powerful to say:

“If you ever drink again, I am leaving forever.”

But if you are not prepared to do that, the statement may create more instability.

Choose consequences that are realistic and connected to the behavior.

“If you begin yelling or threatening me, I will leave the room and stay somewhere else tonight.”

Specific, achievable boundaries are stronger than dramatic ultimatums that disappear later.

Boundaries and Support Can Exist Together

A boundary does not prevent you from offering healthy help.

You might still offer to:

  • Drive them to treatment.
  • Help locate a counselor.
  • Buy food.
  • Attend a family appointment.
  • Watch children during a recovery meeting.
  • Sit with them while they make a call.
  • Listen when the conversation is respectful.
  • Help create a safety plan.
You are available to support recovery.
You are not available to make continued addiction easier.

What If They Break the Boundary?

A boundary is not designed to prevent another person from making a choice.

It explains what happens when they do.

If the boundary is:

“You cannot stay here while using drugs in the house.”

and they use drugs in the house, the next step is not another argument about whether the rule was fair.

The next step is the action you already communicated.

This is often the hardest part.

Following through may feel painful, especially when the person cries, becomes angry, or promises immediate change.

But repeatedly withdrawing a boundary can keep both of you trapped in the same cycle.

What If You Depend on the Person?

Boundaries become more complicated when you share housing, finances, transportation, children, or employment.

You may not be able to simply leave or remove the person from your life.

In those situations, focus on the parts you can control and create a practical plan.

That may include:

  • Separating finances.
  • Protecting important documents.
  • Changing passwords.
  • Identifying somewhere safe to stay.
  • Speaking with a counselor or attorney.
  • Informing a trusted family member.
  • Creating a transportation plan.
  • Documenting serious incidents.
  • Understanding your housing or custody rights.

You do not have to announce every protective step in advance.

Safety planning is different from punishment.

Know When Safety Requires Outside Help

Boundaries alone may not be enough when there is:

  • Physical violence.
  • Threats of violence.
  • Stalking.
  • Weapons.
  • Child endangerment.
  • Impaired driving.
  • Serious property destruction.
  • Overdose.
  • Threats of suicide or homicide.
  • Severe intoxication or unconsciousness.

In an immediate emergency, contact emergency services.

Do not put yourself in physical danger trying to enforce a boundary alone.

Your safety matters.

Frequently Asked Questions

Are boundaries the same as ultimatums?

Not necessarily.

An ultimatum often attempts to force someone to make a specific choice.

A boundary explains what you will do to protect yourself or others when a behavior occurs.

Attempt to control: “You must stop drinking.”

Boundary: “I will not allow drinking in my home.”

What if they say my boundary is unfair?

They are allowed to dislike your boundary.

You are still allowed to decide what you will permit in your home, finances, vehicle, and relationships.

Listen to reasonable concerns, but do not assume disagreement means the limit is wrong.

Should I explain why I am setting the boundary?

A brief explanation can be helpful.

You do not need to present a courtroom case or convince the person to agree.

State the concern, the boundary, and what you will do.

What if I break my own boundary?

Begin again.

Consider why following through was difficult and whether the boundary needs to be adjusted.

You may need practical support from friends, family, a counselor, or another professional.

Can boundaries make someone seek treatment?

They may make the reality of the situation clearer, but there is no guarantee.

The purpose of a boundary is not to manipulate someone into recovery.

It is to protect safety and stop participating in harmful patterns.

Does setting a boundary mean I should cut off contact?

No.

Some people need distance for safety or emotional health, but not every boundary requires ending the relationship.

You can maintain contact while limiting money, access to your home, abusive communication, or unsafe behavior.

You Are Allowed to Protect Your Life

Setting boundaries with someone you love can feel unnatural at first.

You may feel guilty.

You may question yourself.

You may worry that firmness means you have stopped being compassionate.

It does not.

“I care about you, and I care about what this is doing to me.”
“I am willing to support recovery.”
“I am not willing to continue participating in harm.”

Sometimes that clarity is one of the most loving things you can offer.
