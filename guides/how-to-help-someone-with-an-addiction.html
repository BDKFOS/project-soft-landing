Supporting someone you care about

How to Help Someone With an Addiction

Watching someone you love struggle with addiction can leave you feeling helpless, frustrated, angry, and heartbroken—all at the same time. There may not be a perfect script, but there are ways to support someone with compassion while protecting your own well-being.

Start With Compassion, Not Judgment

Addiction is often surrounded by shame. Many people struggling with substance use already know that their choices have affected themselves and the people they care about.

Beginning the conversation with anger, accusations, or blame may cause them to become defensive instead of honest.

That does not mean ignoring harmful behavior. It means leading with concern rather than condemnation.

Instead of saying:

“You are ruining your life.”

Try:

“I am worried about you because I care about you.”

The goal is not to win an argument. The goal is to create an opportunity for an honest conversation.

Choose the Right Time to Talk

Whenever possible, speak with the person when they are not intoxicated and when neither of you is rushing or already in the middle of an argument.

Choose a private setting where both of you can speak without an audience or unnecessary distractions.

You might begin with:

“There is something important I would like to talk about. Is now an okay time?”

Listen More Than You Speak

One of the most meaningful things you can do is listen without immediately interrupting, correcting, or trying to solve every problem.

Listening does not mean agreeing with everything they say. It means trying to understand what they are experiencing.

Helpful questions may include:

  • “How have you been feeling lately?”
  • “What has been the hardest part?”
  • “What do you think would help right now?”
  • “Have you thought about talking with someone?”

Open-ended questions often create more useful conversations than lectures or accusations.

Be Specific About What You Have Seen

Broad statements such as “You have a problem” can quickly turn into an argument about labels.

Instead, describe the specific behavior you have observed and how it has affected you or others.

For example:

  • “I have noticed that you have missed work several times this month after drinking.”
  • “You have stopped spending time with people and doing things you used to enjoy.”
  • “I was frightened when you drove after drinking.”
  • “You asked me for money after spending your paycheck on pills.”

Focus on behavior and impact rather than insults, character judgments, or attempts to diagnose them.

Encourage Help Without Trying to Control Them

You cannot force another person to want recovery. Lasting change usually requires their participation.

What you can do is make the next healthy step feel less overwhelming.

Practical support may include:

  • Helping them locate a therapist or treatment provider.
  • Finding a recovery meeting in their area.
  • Offering a ride to an appointment.
  • Sitting with them while they make a phone call.
  • Helping arrange childcare or pet care while they seek help.
  • Encouraging them to speak with a doctor, counselor, sponsor, or trusted recovery support person.

Offer one or two realistic options rather than overwhelming them with an entire recovery plan.

Understand the Difference Between Helping and Enabling

Helping supports the person as they move toward health and responsibility.

Enabling protects the addiction from consequences and may make continued substance use easier.

Examples of helping

  • Driving someone to treatment.
  • Buying groceries instead of giving cash.
  • Listening without judgment.
  • Helping them contact a counselor.
  • Supporting healthy recovery choices.

Examples of possible enabling

  • Giving cash that is likely to be spent on drugs or alcohol.
  • Lying to an employer about why the person missed work.
  • Repeatedly paying debts caused by substance use.
  • Ignoring unsafe behavior to avoid conflict.
  • Constantly rescuing the person from every consequence.

A helpful question to ask yourself is:

“Does this support recovery, or does it make continued addiction easier?”

Set Healthy Boundaries

Supporting someone does not mean sacrificing your safety, finances, home, or emotional health.

A boundary explains what you will or will not participate in and what action you will take when a limit is crossed.

Money

“I will not give you cash. I can buy groceries or pay a treatment provider directly.”

Your home

“You cannot bring drugs or unsafe people into my home.”

Communication

“I will talk with you when we are both calm. If you threaten or insult me, I will end the conversation.”

Driving

“You may not use my vehicle after drinking or using drugs.”

Children

“I will not leave the children in your care when you are intoxicated.”

Boundaries are not punishments. They are decisions about safety, stability, and what you are realistically willing to accept.

Do Not Make Threats You Cannot Keep

Dramatic ultimatums made during an argument often fall apart once the immediate crisis passes.

Start with one or two boundaries that you are genuinely prepared to maintain.

Consistency is more effective than repeatedly making severe threats and withdrawing them.

Take Care of Yourself Too

Supporting someone through addiction can be emotionally exhausting. You may become so focused on their choices that your own health, sleep, relationships, or responsibilities begin to suffer.

Stay connected with trusted friends and family. Consider speaking with a counselor or joining a support group for people affected by someone else’s substance use.

Taking care of yourself is not selfish. It helps you make clearer decisions and remain emotionally stable during difficult moments.

Know When It Is an Emergency

Call emergency services immediately if someone:

  • Cannot be awakened.
  • Has stopped breathing or is breathing very slowly.
  • Has blue, gray, or unusually pale lips or fingertips.
  • Is having a seizure.
  • Is severely confused or unable to remain conscious.
  • May have overdosed.
  • Is threatening to harm themselves or someone else.

Do not leave the person alone while waiting for help.

If an opioid overdose may have occurred and naloxone is available, administer it according to its instructions.

Frequently Asked Questions

Can I force someone into recovery?

In most situations, you cannot force someone to participate meaningfully in recovery. You can express concern, encourage treatment, offer practical help, and set boundaries around behavior that affects you.

Should I give them money?

If there is a reasonable chance the money may support substance use, consider meeting the need directly instead. You might buy food, arrange transportation, or pay a legitimate provider directly.

What if they refuse help?

Continue communicating honestly without repeatedly arguing. Maintain your boundaries, protect yourself and others, and leave the door open for a future recovery-focused conversation.

Should I confront them while they are intoxicated?

A detailed conversation is usually more productive when the person is as clear-headed as possible. If there is an immediate safety risk, focus on safety rather than trying to resolve the entire problem in that moment.

Is addiction my fault?

No single family member or friend causes another person’s addiction. Relationships and environments can influence people, but you are not responsible for controlling another adult’s substance use or recovery.

You Do Not Have to Fix Everything Today

Supporting someone with an addiction can feel overwhelming because every problem may seem urgent.

You do not need to solve their entire life in one conversation. Focus on the next honest, healthy, and safe step.

You can care deeply, encourage recovery, and remain available without taking responsibility for choices that are not yours.
